The UK-EU food deal in plain English

On 20 November the UK and the EU agreed to share a single rulebook for food, farming and animal health. Here is what has been agreed, what changes on the farm, and what it says about how this country feeds itself.

By Harry Hatfield

On 20 November, the UK and the EU agreed to share a single rulebook for food, farming and animal health. It is the biggest change to British food rules since Brexit, and it starts on 1 March 2028.

Most of the coverage has been written for exporters, lawyers and trade bodies. This is our attempt to explain it in plain English: what has been agreed, what changes on the farm, what it means for the food on our shelves, and what it says about how this country feeds itself.

What has been agreed

In short: Britain will follow EU rules on food, and in return the EU will stop checking British food at the border.

The deal is called a Sanitary and Phytosanitary (SPS) agreement. That is the technical name for rules on food safety and on animal and plant health. Under it, Great Britain will follow EU rules on food safety, animal health and welfare, plant health, pesticides, organics, labelling and marketing standards. It will also keep following them as the EU changes them. This is what people mean by "dynamic alignment".

In return, most of the paperwork and border checks on food moving between Britain and the EU go away. Export health certificates, which cost up to £200 each, will no longer be needed. Routine checks on meat, dairy and fish at the border end. Products the EU has refused since Brexit, such as fresh sausages, burgers and seed potatoes, can be sold there again.

The UK will get an early say in EU rules as they are drafted, but not a vote. Farm subsidies are not part of the deal: British farmers will not get the EU's Common Agricultural Policy payments.

What changes on the farm, and when

The point most farmers will miss: the new rules apply to everything produced in Britain, whether or not it is ever sold to Europe. A farm that has never exported a thing will still be working to the EU rulebook from March 2028.

Several questions are still open, including how the EU's bird flu controls, bovine TB policy and the rules on preventive use of veterinary medicines will be applied here. The detail will come through a European Partnership Bill and a long list of secondary legislation, so farmers should expect more news over the next 15 months.

What it means for food markets

The deal is mainly about making it cheaper and easier to move food across the Channel, in both directions.

For British exporters, it is good news. Three quarters of UK agri-food exports went to the EU in 2025, yet export volumes have fallen by around 35% since Brexit. Less paperwork should win some of that back, particularly for meat and dairy.

The flip side is that European food will flow into Britain more easily too. Supermarkets and wholesalers will find it simpler to buy from the Continent, and British farmers will be competing with EU producers on the same rules but not the same support. The NFU points out that the UK already has the lowest level of farm support in Europe. For commodity producers selling on price into long supply chains, that is real pressure.

Shoppers may see more choice and steadier supply. Whether that helps British farms depends on how much of the value stays with the people who grow the food, and on current form, very little does.

What it tells us about food security

Strip away the trade language and this deal is an admission: Britain cannot comfortably feed itself without Europe.

The figures have been there for years. The EU supplied 64% of the food, feed and drink the UK imported in 2023. For foods we can grow here, Britain produces about 75% of what it eats, down from 95% in 1984. A large share of fresh produce, meat and dairy comes through Dover and the Channel Tunnel. When the queues at those ports grew after Brexit, so did the gaps on supermarket shelves.

So the government has chosen to tie our food rules to Brussels, permanently, to keep that supply line open. That may well be the sensible short-term call. But a country that has to sign up to someone else's rulebook to keep food arriving is not food secure. It is food dependent.

The answer is not to pull up the drawbridge. It is to rebuild the capacity to produce, process and sell food close to where it is eaten, so that a queue at Dover or a rule change in Brussels is an inconvenience, not a crisis.

Where local producers stand

We will work to the new rules like every other farm in Britain. Much of what governs on-farm poultry processing already comes from EU law, and our welfare and hygiene standards already sit well above the minimum. What the deal does not touch is how our model works.

ZMMC farms do not export. Our birds are reared on pasture, processed on the farm that raised them and sold to people who live nearby. There is no border to cross, no export certificate to save on and no Channel crossing to queue for. When food markets swing because of a trade deal, a price war or a blocked port, a farm that sells direct to its own community feels it far less.

That is the point of building decentralised. Every ZMMC franchise is a small piece of food security that does not depend on a lorry from Europe. The more of them there are, the less this country has to trade away to keep its shelves full.
