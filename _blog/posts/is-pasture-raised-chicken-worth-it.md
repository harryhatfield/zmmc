The question I get asked most, at the farm gate and in every chef's kitchen I walk into, is a fair one: why does your chicken cost so much more than the supermarket's?

I co-founded The Zero Miles Meat Company with Maciej Rudz after building the system on our own farm, Berkshire Broilers in Wokingham. We have reared, processed and sold every bird ourselves, so I can give you a straight answer from the field rather than a sales line. The short version is that you are paying for time, space, labour and a processing room on the farm itself. Whether that is worth it depends on how much of the bird you use.

## In short

- Whole pasture-raised birds typically sell for £15 to £30, against roughly £4 to £7 for a standard supermarket chicken.
- Most of the extra cost comes from time. Our birds are processed at nine to twelve weeks old. A standard broiler is ready in around six.
- Used properly, one whole bird gives 8 to 12 portions, which closes much of the gap per plate.
- Buying whole, direct from the farm or through its local butcher, is usually the best value per kilo.
- Chefs and butchers should budget on yield and menu price, not the headline cost per kilo.

## How the prices compare

A whole pasture-raised chicken typically sells for £15 to £30. Free-range sits at around £8 to £14 and a standard indoor bird at £4 to £7. These are typical UK market ranges, not our prices. Every farm in the ZMMC network sets its own, so the figure you see will depend on the bird's weight, your area and where you buy it.

| Whole bird | Typical price | What the label tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Standard indoor | £4 to £7 | Housed indoors, fast-growing breed |
| Free-range | £8 to £14 | Meets the legal outdoor access rules |
| Pasture-raised | £15 to £30 | Kept on open grass, usually slower-growing. No legal definition. |

That last point matters. "Standard" and "free-range" are controlled terms under the UK's [poultry meat marketing standards](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/poultry-meat-marketing-standards), and the free-range rules are still being refined, as the [2025 free-range amendment debate](https://hansard.parliament.uk/lords/2025-09-10/debates/ABF50ED8-6E9F-40CF-99D9-288C44997A0B/Free-RangePoultrymeatMarketingStandards%28Amendment%29%28England%29Regulations2025) shows. "Pasture-raised" is not defined in law at all. A free-range bird can meet the outdoor access minimum without spending much of its life on grass.

Because there is no legal standard, we wrote our own. Every farm in our network works to the ZMMC Rearing Policy, which sets out the breed, the brooding period, how often birds move and how long fields rest. If you are buying pasture-raised from anyone, ask them what theirs is.

> **Comparing quotes**
>
> - **Price per kilo, not per bird.** A £22 bird at 2.4kg is better value than an £18 bird at 1.8kg.
> - **Whole or prepared.** Portioned and spatchcocked birds carry extra butchery labour.
> - **What "pasture-raised" means here.** How old were the birds, how long were they outdoors, and on what ground?

## Why it costs more to produce

Every bird lives longer, eats more, needs more land and more hands, and is processed in a small batch on the farm rather than on a factory line. Almost every cost on the farm scales with time, and time is the one thing we will not cut.

### How a bird is raised on a ZMMC farm

The four stages every franchisee farm follows under our Rearing Policy:

1. **Brood.** Day-old chicks spend two to four weeks in a warm, gas-heated brooder on hemp bedding, depending on the weather.
2. **Pasture.** They then go straight out to grass in flock tractors of 60, moved onto fresh ground every day, with shade cover and electric fox protection.
3. **Process.** At nine to twelve weeks they are processed in a purpose-built container on the same farm, by trained, licensed operators. No live haulage.
4. **Sell.** The birds go to local butchers, farm shops, restaurants and direct customers in that farm's own area.

### Time on the farm

A standard broiler reaches slaughter weight in around 35 to 42 days. Our farms rear Hubbard breeds from the Better Chicken Commitment list, which grow more slowly and are processed at nine to twelve weeks. That is close to double the feeding period, and slower-growing birds turn feed into meat less efficiently, so the feed cost per kilo rises with it.

![Two white chickens foraging in long green grass.](birds-on-grass.jpg "Birds out on grass: foraging is what the extra weeks are for.")

Longer rearing also means:

- **Fewer batches a year** from the same land and kit, so fixed costs fall on fewer birds.
- **More days exposed** to weather, foxes and disease, which raises the cost of each bird that makes it.
- **More daily work**: checks, water runs and a tractor move for every flock, every day.

We also only rear from March to November. If the birds can't be outside, we don't raise them. That keeps the welfare honest, but it means each farm earns its year in nine months.

### Land, housing and labour

Birds on pasture need ground that can rest and recover. On our farms a field rests for at least 45 days before birds return to it, and stocking density is low by design. An indoor shed with automated feeding needs none of that land and a fraction of the labour.

The feed itself is a British-farmed ration from Harpers Feeds with no additives. Birds are raised without antibiotics. If a bird ever needs veterinary treatment, it is removed from the flock that goes to market.

If you see bare, muddy runs, crowded shelters or birds that rarely go out, you are looking at a system wearing the label without paying the cost behind it.

### Processing on the farm

This is where small producers carry the steepest cost per bird, and it is the problem ZMMC was set up to solve. Most UK poultry is driven to large, centralised plants that spread equipment, inspection and staff across enormous volumes. For a small farm, access to processing is one of the biggest barriers there is.

![A dark green shipping container carrying the gold Zero Miles Meat Company roundel.](processing-container.jpg "The ZMMC processing container: a complete processing room, built to sit on the farm where the birds are raised.")

Every ZMMC farm processes its own birds in a purpose-built 40ft container, sized for up to around 10,000 birds a season. Each operator holds a Welfare at the Time of Killing licence, every site has its own HACCP plan, and the set-up is signed off by the local Environmental Health team. The birds never travel. That removes the stress of live transport and keeps traceability simple: one farm, one flock, one label.

It still takes trained people, careful hygiene and a proper cold chain. Doing that for hundreds of birds a week rather than hundreds of thousands costs more per carcass, and that shows in the price.

## Is it worth it per portion?

**Usually yes, if you use the whole bird.** Our birds are reared to a target dressed weight of around 2.4kg. A bird that size gives 8 to 12 portions across a roast, leftovers and stock. At £15 to £30 a bird, that is roughly £1.25 to £3.75 a portion.

| Whole bird | Typical price | Rough cost per portion |
|---|---|---|
| Standard indoor | £4 to £7 | £0.33 to £0.88 |
| Free-range | £8 to £14 | £0.67 to £1.75 |
| Pasture-raised | £15 to £30 | £1.25 to £3.75 |

These are rough figures. Standard birds are often lighter and may not stretch to 12 portions. But the gap per plate is much smaller than the gap per bird. For a family it might be a pound or two a meal. For a chef plating 40 covers it adds up, which is why yield matters more in a kitchen than at home.

### Where the value gets lost

The sums fall apart when only the breasts and legs get eaten. A roast-only bird gives four to six servings, which roughly doubles the real cost of each plate.

> *If the carcass goes in the bin instead of the stockpot, you have paid pasture-raised prices for half a bird.*

To get the full 8 to 12 portions:

1. Joint or carve the bird and portion it before serving, not at the table.
2. Strip the remaining meat for a second meal, such as a pie, salad or broth.
3. Simmer the carcass for stock and freeze it in measured batches.

### What the money buys beyond the plate

Per-portion maths is only half the answer. Most UK chicken comes from fast-growing breeds kept indoors, as the [RSPCA's overview of broiler farming](https://www.rspca.org.uk/adviceandwelfare/farm/meatchickens) explains. Part of what you pay for with a pasture-raised bird is how it lived, how it died and who got paid for it. When you buy from a ZMMC farm, the margin stays with the farmer who raised the bird, in your own area, rather than disappearing into a long supply chain.

## What changes the price you pay

Three things move the price: how heavy the bird is, whether you buy it whole or in pieces, and where you buy it. Whole birds almost always work out cheaper per kilo than the same meat sold as cuts.

### Bird size

Heavier birds cost more at the till, but the price per kilo usually holds steady or drops slightly. Some sellers price per bird in size bands, others weigh each one and charge per kilo. Check whether the weight includes giblets and neck, which add weight without adding roasting meat.

### Whole bird or cuts

Across the market, cuts tend to be priced in the same order per kilo. Breast fillet costs most, because it is in highest demand and takes the most labour. Legs and thighs come well below that and are often better for slow cooking. Wings are cheaper again, and frames for stock are cheapest of all. Add the parts back up and you usually pay more than for the whole bird. If you only ever want breast, cuts may still suit you. If you will use everything, buy whole.

### Questions to ask whoever sells it

1. **Kill date.** When was it processed, and what is the use-by? At Berkshire Broilers we process on a Tuesday and deliver on the Wednesday.
2. **Chilling.** Was it air-chilled or water-chilled? Water chilling can add retained water to the weight you pay for.
3. **Storage.** Has it been chilled throughout, or frozen and thawed?
4. **Weight basis.** Per bird or per kilo, and are giblets included?
5. **Availability.** Are birds reared in batches, and can you order ahead?

Straight answers to those five usually tell you whether a higher price reflects a better bird.

## Budgeting for chefs, butchers and farm shops

Trade buyers should budget per finished portion and per week of supply, not per bird. Buyers who plan around the whole carcass and the farm's season usually find a pasture-raised bird fits their margin. Buyers who treat it like a commodity breast fillet usually don't.

### Build the plan around the whole bird

- **Prime cuts:** breasts and legs for the signature dish or the premium counter line.
- **Secondary use:** wings and thigh trim for bar snacks, pies or ready meals.
- **Frame and giblets:** stock, gravy and sauces, replacing bought-in bases.

The mistake to avoid is buying whole birds and only using the breasts. That roughly doubles the true cost of every portion you sell.

### Plan for the season and for weight

Our birds come off grass in batches, so plan orders around the farm's rearing cycle rather than expecting next-day top-ups. Supply runs from spring to late autumn, so talk to your farm early about the months you need most.

Birds also vary a little from batch to batch around the 2.4kg target. Budget on a weight band rather than a fixed figure, and agree with your farm before the first delivery whether you are paying per bird or per kilo.

> **A tip for menu pricing**
>
> Set your menu or counter price on the average portion cost across several batches, not on the cheapest delivery. One lighter batch should never wipe out your margin.

### Protect the premium you charge

Customers pay more when they trust what they are told. Make sure the description you use matches how the birds were actually reared before you put it on a menu or a shelf ticket. Name the farm, brief your front-of-house and counter staff so they can explain why the birds grow slower, and keep your supplier paperwork on file in case a customer or inspector asks. Every ZMMC farm can tell you exactly which flock your birds came from.

## Is it worth producing for farmers?

**It can be, if you have grass you can rotate, the labour for daily moves, and local buyers lined up before your first chicks arrive.** It sits well alongside livestock, arable, equestrian or diversified enterprises, and it does not need extensive buildings.

![A farmer in a green ZMMC polo crouching on grass beside white chickens.](farmer-with-flock.jpg "Out on pasture with the flock.")

### What a ZMMC farm needs

- Around 10 acres of free-draining pasture you can rotate
- Access to water
- Space for the processing container, plus storage for feed and a brooding area
- Three-phase mains power, or a generator of at least 22kVA

Free-draining ground matters more than acreage. Waterlogged fields foul quickly and raise disease pressure, and overstocking a small area turns grass to mud within a batch.

### Approvals

Slaughter and processing on farm are regulated, and the paperwork is what stops most farmers before they start. You will need registration with your local authority, Welfare at the Time of Killing competence for whoever does the killing, a HACCP-based food safety plan and a compliant route for animal by-products. Approvals take time, so start early.

## Questions we get asked

**Why is pasture-raised chicken so expensive?**
The birds live roughly twice as long as a standard broiler, eat more feed over that time, need more land and daily labour, and are processed in small batches on the farm rather than on an industrial line. Used whole, a bird gives 8 to 12 portions, which narrows the gap per meal.

**Is Zero Miles chicken organic?**
No. Birds on ZMMC farms are not certified organic. They eat a British-farmed feed with no additives, are raised without antibiotics, and go out onto grass after two to four weeks in the brooder.

**Why can I only buy it from March to November?**
If the birds can't be outside, we don't raise them. Our farms rear seasonally, so it is worth stocking your freezer in the autumn.

**Can chickens be 100% grass-fed?**
No. Chickens are omnivores. On pasture they forage for grass, seeds and insects, but they still need a balanced feed to stay healthy. "Pasture-raised" describes where and how a bird lives, not a grass-only diet.

**Does it taste different from supermarket chicken?**
Most people notice a deeper flavour and a firmer texture, because slower-growing birds that move about outdoors develop more muscle. It can benefit from slightly gentler or longer cooking than a fast-grown bird.

**Where can I buy chicken from a ZMMC farm?**
Direct from the farm, or through the local butchers, farm shops and restaurants each farm supplies. Berkshire Broilers in Wokingham is our Founding Franchisee Farm, and new farms will join the network from 2027.
